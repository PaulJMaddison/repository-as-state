\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

RaS changes the locus of persistence. Instead of beginning with the goal of keeping an intelligent process alive indefinitely, it asks what durable engineering state allows a fresh process to act correctly. This inversion does not eliminate memory; it moves the research focus from preserving the agent's complete trajectory toward preserving and reconstructing authoritative consequences of engineering work.

\subsection{Observed origin of the architecture}

The architecture was recognised after repeated practical software-engineering use rather than designed purely theoretically. The observed workflow involved human orchestration and problem selection, high-capability AI reasoning, detailed bounded engineering specifications, comparatively shallow local execution agents, deterministic build/test/runtime evidence, repository persistence, and later fresh reasoning sessions reconstructing state.

That origin is useful because it identifies a concrete systems pattern worth testing. It also creates a risk of confirmation bias: a workflow that felt effective can motivate hypotheses but cannot validate them. The claims register therefore classifies the origin as \textbf{OBSERVED}, not \textbf{EMPIRICAL}.

\subsection{Decision-relevant state rather than transcript preservation}

Engineering work often compresses a large reasoning trajectory into a smaller durable result. A debugging session can contain many hypotheses and failed attempts, while the durable consequence may be a fix, regression test, schema change, and concise design note. RaS predicts that future action often needs these consequences more than it needs the full transcript.

This form of compression is intentionally lossy. The question is not whether history can be reproduced, but whether future correct action changes materially when history is removed. If important rationale disappears, forced reset should expose the loss. If all necessary constraints are present in durable state, preserving the transcript may add little marginal value.

\subsection{Human and AI capability complementarity}

The paper does not depend on a philosophical claim that humans are “real intelligence” while AI systems only pattern match. A more useful framing is that humans and AI systems exhibit different capability profiles.

Human strengths may include persistent personal goals, consequence-grounded experience, strategic problem selection, taste, risk judgement, cross-domain lived experience, and deciding which outcomes matter. High-capability AI may contribute broad technical synthesis, formalisation, rapid generation, multi-domain knowledge, mathematical manipulation, critique, and implementation leverage. Execution workers contribute machine interaction. Deterministic systems add executable truth checks.

A useful workflow is therefore:
\[
\text{human orchestrates}
\rightarrow
\text{AI reasons/amplifies}
\rightarrow
\text{execution worker acts}
\rightarrow
\text{tests/data/reality adjudicate}.
\]
The final arbiter is external evidence. This is a discussion of the observed architecture, not an empirical comparison of human and AI intelligence.

\subsection{When persistent agents may be better}

RaS should not be interpreted as an argument that all agents should reset. Persistent agents may be superior when relevant state is inherently interpersonal, weakly represented in the repository, expensive to reconstruct, or continuously changing outside version control. They may also reduce latency for tightly coupled sequences where the next task begins immediately and history is small.

Hybrid designs are therefore plausible. A system might keep a reasoner alive within a bounded work package while using repository state as the recovery boundary between packages. It might persist selected semantic memories only when the retention principle in Section~\ref{sec:durable-docs} suggests future value. Or it might preserve a session while requiring that every accepted decision still be externalised so the project remains recoverable.

\subsection{What a negative result would teach}

If reset agents consistently fail despite good repository hygiene, that would identify information that repositories systematically fail to preserve. If success depends strongly on explicit state documents, the result may imply that source and tests alone are insufficient. If reconstruction cost grows with dependency width, better indexes or state compilers may be needed. If persistent history helps only for particular task classes, lifecycle routing may be more useful than universal statelessness.

The scientific value of RaS therefore does not depend on a positive result. The research programme is useful if it reveals where engineering continuity actually resides and what it costs to reconstruct.
